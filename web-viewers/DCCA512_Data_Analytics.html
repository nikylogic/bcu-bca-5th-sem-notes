Q4. What is meant by Correlation?

Correlation is a bivariate statistical metric that quantifies the degree (strength) and direction of a linear relationship between two continuous variables (\(X\) and \(Y\)). It is most commonly measured using the Pearson Correlation Coefficient (\(r\)), which is bounded between \(-1\) and \(+1\):
\[r = \frac{\sum (X - \bar{X})(Y - \bar{Y})}{\sqrt{\sum (X - \bar{X})^2 \sum (Y - \bar{Y})^2}}\]
Interpretation:
  • \(r = +1\): Perfect positive linear relationship.
  • \(r = -1\): Perfect negative linear relationship.
  • \(r = 0\): No linear relationship.
(Note: Correlation implies association, not causation.)

Q5. What is Regression?

Regression is a supervised statistical modeling technique used to evaluate and describe the mathematical relationship between a dependent variable (\(Y\), target/response) and one or more independent variables (\(X\), predictors/features). Its primary objectives are prediction, forecasting, and inferring causal effects.

Simple Linear Regression Equation:
\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \epsilon\]
(where \(\beta_0\) is the \(Y\)-intercept, \(\beta_1\) is the slope coefficient, and \(\epsilon\) is the random error term).

    Example: For tossing a fair coin once, \(S = \{\text{Heads}, \text{Tails}\}\).
Concept Domain Key Characteristic

Q7. Define conditional probability.

Conditional Probability is the probability of an event \(A\) occurring given that another event \(B\) has already occurred. It is denoted as \(P(A \mid B)\) and is mathematically defined as the ratio of the joint probability of both events to the marginal probability of the conditioning event, provided \(P(B) > 0\):
\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad \text{where } P(B) > 0\]
Property: If events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, then \(P(A \mid B) = P(A)\).

Q3. What is Positive and Negative Correlation?

Correlation quantifies the direction and strength of the linear association between two variables (\(X\) and \(Y\)):
  • Positive Correlation (\(r > 0\)): Occurs when both variables move in the same direction. As variable \(X\) increases, variable \(Y\) also increases; as \(X\) decreases, \(Y\) also decreases (e.g., study hours and exam performance; height and weight). Q4. What is a Regression Coefficient?

A Regression Coefficient is a statistical parameter in a regression equation that measures the expected average rate of change in the dependent variable per one-unit change in the independent variable.

In bivariate linear regression, there are two regression coefficients:
  1. Regression coefficient of \(Y\) on \(X\) (\(b_{yx}\)): Slope of the regression line of \(Y\) on \(X\):
    \[b_{yx} = r \frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x} = \frac{\sum(X - \bar{X})(Y - \bar{Y})}{\sum(X - \bar{X})^2} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_x^2}\]
  2. Regression coefficient of \(X\) on \(Y\) (\(b_{xy}\)): Slope of the regression line of \(X\) on \(Y\):
    \[b_{xy} = r \frac{\sigma_x}{\sigma_y} = \frac{\sum(X - \bar{X})(Y - \bar{Y})}{\sum(Y - \bar{Y})^2} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_y^2}\]
Fundamental Property: The correlation coefficient (\(r\)) is the geometric mean of the two regression coefficients:
\[r = \pm \sqrt{b_{yx} \cdot b_{xy}}\]
(Both regression coefficients and \(r\) must always share the same algebraic sign).

Q6. Define a random variable.

\[X: S \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\]

Q7. Define Binomial Distribution.

A Binomial Distribution is a discrete probability distribution that models the probability of obtaining exactly \(k\) successes in \(n\) independent and identical Bernoulli trials, with a constant probability of success \(p\) in each trial and probability of failure \(q = (1 - p)\).

Probability Mass Function (PMF):
\[P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k q^{n-k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}, \quad \text{for } k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n\]
Key Parameters & Properties:
  • Parameters: \(n\) (number of trials) and \(p\) (probability of success).
  • Mean: \(\mu = np\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2 = npq\)
  • Condition: \(\text{Mean} > \text{Variance}\) (since \(q < 1\)).

Q4. What is Probable Error of the coefficient of correlation?

Probable Error (P.E.) is a statistical measure used to test the reliability, sampling stability, and statistical significance of the calculated Pearson correlation coefficient (\(r\)) in a sample of size \(N\).

Mathematical Formula:
\[P.E. = 0.6745 \times \frac{1 - r^2}{\sqrt{N}}\]
(where \(r\) is the correlation coefficient, \(N\) is the total number of pairs of observations, and \(0.6745 \approx \frac{2}{3}\) of the standard error).

Rules for Interpretation & Significance:
  • If \(|r| < \text{P.E.}\), there is no evidence of correlation (the correlation is statistically insignificant).
  • If \(|r| > 6 \times \text{P.E.}\), the correlation is definitely significant and reliable.
  • The limits of the true population correlation coefficient are given by: \(r \pm \text{P.E.}\)

Q7. Define hypothesis testing.

Hypothesis Testing is a formal, inferential statistical procedure used to determine whether there is sufficient empirical evidence in a sample of data to infer that a certain condition holds true for the entire target population.

Core Procedure: It involves formulating two opposing statistical hypotheses (Null \(H_0\) and Alternative \(H_1\)), selecting an appropriate significance level (\(\alpha\)), computing a test statistic (\(z\), \(t\), \(F\), or \(\chi^2\)), and comparing the resulting test statistic / \(p\)-value against a critical value to decide whether to reject \(H_0\) in favor of \(H_1\).

Q3. If \(b_{xy} = 0.8\) and \(b_{yx} = 0.6\), find \(r\).

Given:
  • Regression coefficient of \(X\) on \(Y\): \(b_{xy} = 0.8\)
  • Regression coefficient of \(Y\) on \(X\): \(b_{yx} = 0.6\)

\[r = \pm \sqrt{b_{xy} \cdot b_{yx}}\]

\[r = \sqrt{0.8 \times 0.6} = \sqrt{0.48} \approx +0.6928\]

Q4. What is Simple Correlation?

Simple Correlation is a statistical measure that studies and quantifies the mutual relationship, co-variation, and association between only two variables at a time (one independent variable and one dependent variable, e.g., height and weight, or advertising expenditure and sales revenue).

Key Distinction: Unlike Multiple Correlation (which studies three or more variables simultaneously) or Partial Correlation (which studies two variables while controlling the effect of others), simple correlation isolates only the pairwise association between two variables.

Metric: Evaluated using the Pearson correlation coefficient (\(r\)) or Spearman rank correlation (\(\rho\)).

Q5. What is Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient?

Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient (denoted by \(\rho\) or \(r_s\)) is a non-parametric statistical measure that quantifies the strength and direction of monotonic association between two ranked or ordinal variables.

Mathematical Formula (without ties):
\[\rho = 1 - \frac{6 \sum d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}\]
(where \(d_i = R_{x_i} - R_{y_i}\) is the difference between ranks assigned to each pair of observations, and \(n\) is the total number of paired observations).

Range & Interpretation: \(-1 \le \rho \le +1\)
  • \(\rho = +1\): Perfect agreement in rankings.
  • \(\rho = -1\): Perfect disagreement (complete inversion) in rankings.
  • \(\rho = 0\): No association between ranks.
Advantage: Ideal for qualitative attributes (e.g., beauty contests, honesty, intelligence) where variables cannot be measured quantitatively but can be ranked.

Q6. Define Poisson Distribution.

A Poisson Distribution is a discrete probability distribution that expresses the probability of a given number of rare events (\(k\)) occurring within a fixed interval of time, area, distance, or volume, given that these events occur independently at a known constant average rate (\(\lambda\)).

Probability Mass Function (PMF):
\[P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \quad \text{for } k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\]
(where \(\lambda > 0\) is the average event rate, \(e \approx 2.71828\), and \(k!\) is the factorial of \(k\)).

Fundamental Property:
\[\text{Mean} = \text{Variance} = \lambda\]
(The equality of mean and variance is the unique characterizing property of a Poisson distribution).

Q7. State any four properties of Normal Distribution.

Four essential mathematical and graphical properties of a Normal (Gaussian) Distribution are:
  1. Symmetry & Bell-Shaped Curve: The probability curve is perfectly symmetrical and bell-shaped about the vertical axis passing through the mean (\(\mu\)), with zero skewness (\(\beta_1 = 0\)) and mesokurtic kurtosis (\(\beta_2 = 3\)).
  2. Coincidence of Central Tendencies: The mean, median, and mode of the distribution are identical and coincide at the exact center:
    \[\text{Mean} = \text{Median} = \text{Mode} = \mu\]
  3. Total Area Under the Curve: The total area bounded by the normal curve and the horizontal axis is exactly equal to unity (\(1\) or \(100\%\)), with \(0.5\) area lying on each side of the mean.
  4. Empirical Rule (Three-Sigma Limits):
    • \(\mu \pm 1\sigma\) encloses \(68.27\%\) of all observations.
    • \(\mu \pm 2\sigma\) encloses \(95.45\%\) of all observations.
    • \(\mu \pm 3\sigma\) encloses \(99.73\%\) of all observations.

Q8. What is ANOVA?

ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) is an inferential statistical technique developed by Sir R. A. Fisher used to test whether there are statistically significant differences among the means of three or more independent groups simultaneously, without inflating the Type I error rate.

Underlying Principle: It partitions the total variation observed in the dataset into two components:
  1. Between-Group Variance (\(SSB\)): Variation attributable to differences between the treatment groups.
  2. Within-Group Variance (\(SSW\) / Error Variance): Random variation or error within individual groups.
Test Statistic (\(F\)-ratio):
\[F = \frac{\text{Mean Square Between (MSB)}}{\text{Mean Square Within (MSW)}} = \frac{SSB / df_b}{SSW / df_w}\]
If calculated \(F > F_{\text{critical}}\), the null hypothesis (\(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_k\)) is rejected.

Q3. If \(r = 0.6\) and \(N = 64\), find probable error.

Given:
  • Pearson correlation coefficient: \(r = 0.6\)
  • Sample size: \(N = 64\)
Formula:
\[\text{P.E.} = 0.6745 \times \frac{1 - r^2}{\sqrt{N}}\]
Step-by-Step Calculation:
  1. \(r^2 = (0.6)^2 = 0.36\)
  2. \(1 - r^2 = 1 - 0.36 = 0.64\)
  3. \(\sqrt{N} = \sqrt{64} = 8\)
  4. \(\frac{1 - r^2}{\sqrt{N}} = \frac{0.64}{8} = 0.08\)
  5. \(\text{P.E.} = 0.6745 \times 0.08 = 0.05396 \approx 0.054\)
Significance Test:
\[6 \times \text{P.E.} = 6 \times 0.05396 = 0.32376\]
Since \(r = 0.6 > 6 \times \text{P.E.}\) (\(0.6 > 0.3238\)), the correlation is statistically significant and highly reliable.

Q5. What is Chi-Square Test?

The Chi-Square (\(\chi^2\)) Test is a non-parametric statistical hypothesis test developed by Karl Pearson used to determine whether there is a statistically significant difference between expected frequencies and observed frequencies across one or more categorical variables.

Mathematical Formula:
\[\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}\]
(where \(O_i\) is the Observed frequency, \(E_i\) is the Expected frequency, and \(\sum\) denotes summation over all cells/categories).

Two Major Applications:
  1. Test of Goodness of Fit: Tests whether observed sample data conforms to a theoretical probability distribution.
  2. Test of Independence of Attributes: Determines whether two categorical variables organized in a contingency table are statistically independent (e.g., testing if vaccination status is independent of disease incidence).

Q6. Define probability.

Probability is a mathematical measure of the likelihood, chance, or degree of certainty that a specific event will occur as a result of a random experiment.

Classical (Mathematical) Definition: If a random experiment results in \(n(S)\) mutually exclusive, exhaustive, and equally likely outcomes, and \(n(E)\) of these outcomes are favorable to the occurrence of event \(E\), the probability of event \(E\) is defined as:
\[P(E) = \frac{n(E)}{n(S)} = \frac{\text{Number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of possible outcomes}}\]
Axiomatic Range: For any event \(E\), its probability strictly satisfies:
\[0 \le P(E) \le 1\]
(where \(P(E) = 0\) represents an impossible event, and \(P(E) = 1\) represents a certain event).

Q7. Define probability distribution.

A Probability Distribution is a mathematical function or rule that describes the probabilities of all possible values or outcomes that a random variable can take within a specified sample space.

Classification:
  1. Discrete Probability Distribution: Defined for discrete random variables, where probabilities are assigned to isolated values using a Probability Mass Function (PMF), satisfying \(P(X = x_i) \ge 0\) and \(\sum P(X = x_i) = 1\) (e.g., Binomial, Poisson).
  2. Continuous Probability Distribution: Defined for continuous random variables, where probabilities over intervals are modeled using a continuous curve known as a Probability Density Function (PDF), satisfying \(f(x) \ge 0\) and \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) dx = 1\) (e.g., Normal distribution).

Q8. What is a t-test? When is a t-test generally used?

A t-test (Student's t-test, formulated by William Sealy Gosset) is a parametric inferential statistical test used to determine whether there is a statistically significant difference between the means of two groups, or between a sample mean and a known population mean.

Formula (One-Sample t-test):
\[t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{s / \sqrt{n}}, \quad \text{with } df = n - 1\]
(where \(\bar{x}\) is sample mean, \(\mu\) is population mean, \(s\) is sample standard deviation, and \(n\) is sample size).

When a t-test is generally used:
  1. When the sample size is small (\(n < 30\)).
  2. When the true population standard deviation (\(\sigma\)) is unknown and estimated using the sample standard deviation (\(s\)).
  3. When the underlying population from which the sample is drawn is approximately normally distributed.

\[P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x q^{n - x}, \quad \text{where } \binom{n}{x} = \frac{n!}{x!(n - x)!}\]

Q14. Explain Classical, Empirical, and Axiomatic approaches to probability.

1. Definition (1 Mark)

Probability quantifies the likelihood of occurrence of random events, conceptualized through deductive symmetry (Classical), relative frequency (Empirical), or set-theoretic foundations (Axiomatic).

2. Comparative Approaches Table (2 Marks)

Approach Formulation Principle Mathematical Formula Underlying Condition
Classical Deductive symmetry \(P(A) = \frac{m}{n}\) Finite, equiprobable outcomes.
Empirical Relative frequency \(P(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f_n(A)}{n}\) Repeatable independent trials.
Axiomatic Measure & set theory Kolmogorov Axioms Non-negativity, \(P(S)=1\), Additivity.

3. Key Points (2-3 Marks)

  • Classical (Laplace): Computes \(P(A) = m/n\) a priori; flawed by circularity ("equally likely") and fails for biased or continuous events.
  • Empirical (von Mises): Computes probability a posteriori as limiting relative frequency; limited because infinite trials are impossible.
  • Axiomatic (Kolmogorov): Defines probability as a real-valued function \(P: \mathcal{F} \to \mathbb{R}\) on sample space \(S\) satisfying:
  • Non-negativity: \(P(A) \ge 0\).
  • Certainty: \(P(S) = 1\).
  • Additivity: For disjoint events, \(P(\bigcup A_i) = \sum P(A_i)\).
  • Universal Rigor: Unifies classical and empirical models across discrete and continuous distributions.

4. Example (1 Mark)

  • Example: Fair die: Classical \(P(\text{Even}) = 3/6 = 0.5\); Empirical: 10,000 rolls with ratio \(\frac{4,992}{10,000} \approx 0.499\).

(Given: Critical t-value for 15 degrees of freedom at the 5% level of significance for a two-tailed test = 2.131).

1. Given Data

  • Hypothesized Population Mean \(\mu_0 = 50 \text{ hours}\).
  • Sample Size \(n = 16\) (\(n < 30 \implies \text{Small Sample Student's } t\text{-test}\)).
  • Sample Mean \(\bar{x} = 54 \text{ hours}\).
  • Sample Standard Deviation \(s = 8 \text{ hours}\).
  • Level of Significance \(\alpha = 0.05\) (5%).
  • Degrees of Freedom \(df = n - 1 = 16 - 1 = 15\).
  • Two-tailed Critical Value: \(t_{\text{critical}} = 2.131\).

2. Hypothesis Formulation

  • Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): \(\mu = 50\)
    (The average battery life is not significantly different from the manufacturer's claim of 50 hours).
  • Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_1\)): \(\mu \neq 50\)
    (Two-tailed test: The average battery life is significantly different from 50 hours).

3. Test Statistic Formula (Student's Single-Sample \(t\)-Test)

\[t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{SE_{\bar{x}}} = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}}\]

4. Step-by-Step Calculation

    \[SE_{\bar{x}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{8}{\sqrt{16}} = \frac{8}{4} = 2.0 \text{ hours}\]

  2. Compute Calculated \(t\)-Statistic:

    \[t_{\text{calculated}} = \frac{54 - 50}{2.0} = \frac{4}{2.0} = +2.000\]
    \[|t_{\text{calculated}}| = 2.000\]

5. Statistical Decision Rule & Conclusion

  • Decision Rule: For a two-tailed test at \(\alpha = 0.05\) with \(df = 15\):
  • If \(|t_{\text{calculated}}| > t_{\text{critical}} \implies\) Reject \(H_0\).

  • If \(|t_{\text{calculated}}| \le t_{\text{critical}} \implies\) Fail to reject (Accept) \(H_0\).

  • Comparison:

    \[|t_{\text{calculated}}| = 2.000 < t_{\text{critical}} = 2.131\]

  • Statistical Inference: Since the absolute calculated value of \(t\) (\(2.000\)) is strictly less than the critical tabulated value (\(2.131\)), we fail to reject (accept) the Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)) at the 5% significance level.

(Given: Critical \(t\)-value for 18 degrees of freedom at the 5% level of significance for a two-tailed test = 2.101).

Solution:

1. Formulation of Statistical Hypotheses

  • Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): \(\mu_1 = \mu_2\)
    (There is no statistically significant difference between the mean performance of students taught using Method A and Method B).
  • Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_1\)): \(\mu_1 \neq \mu_2\)
    (There is a significant difference between the two teaching methods — two-tailed test).

2. Degrees of Freedom (\(df\))

\[df = n_1 + n_2 - 2 = 10 + 10 - 2 = 18\]

3. Pooled Sample Variance (\(s_p^2\)) & Standard Error (\(SE\))

Since population variances are assumed equal (\(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\)), we compute the pooled sample variance:

\[s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\]

Substitute given values:

\[s_p^2 = \frac{(10 - 1)(5^2) + (10 - 1)(4^2)}{10 + 10 - 2} = \frac{9(25) + 9(16)}{18}\]
\[s_p^2 = \frac{225 + 144}{18} = \frac{369}{18} = 20.5\]

  • Standard Error of the Difference Between Means (\(SE\)):
    \[SE = \sqrt{s_p^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)} = \sqrt{20.5 \left(\frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{10}\right)}\]
    \[SE = \sqrt{20.5 \times \frac{2}{10}} = \sqrt{20.5 \times 0.2} = \sqrt{4.1} \approx 2.0248\]

4. Computation of Test Statistic (\(t_{\text{calculated}}\))

\[t_{\text{cal}} = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{SE} = \frac{74 - 68}{2.0248} = \frac{6}{2.0248} \approx 2.9632\]

5. Statistical Decision Rule & Conclusion

  • Calculated Value: \(|t_{\text{cal}}| = 2.9632\)
  • Critical / Table Value (\(t_{\text{tab}}\)): At \(\alpha = 0.05\) for \(df = 18\) (two-tailed test), \(t_{\text{crit}} = 2.101\).

\[|t_{\text{cal}}| = 2.9632 > t_{\text{crit}} = 2.101\]

  • Decision: Since the absolute calculated \(t\)-statistic exceeds the critical value, we reject the null hypothesis (\(H_0\)) at the \(5\%\) level of significance.
  • Conclusion: There is a statistically significant difference between the mean marks of students taught by the two methods. Students taught under Method A (\(\bar{X}_1 = 74\)) performed significantly better than those under Method B (\(\bar{X}_2 = 68\)).

6. Final Boxed Summary

\[t_{\text{calculated}} = 2.9632, \quad t_{\text{critical}} = 2.101\]

\[\text{Decision: Reject } H_0 \quad (\text{Significant difference exists})\]

    \[\sum Y = 5 + 6 + 7 + 9 + 13 = 40 \implies \bar{Y} = \frac{\sum Y}{N} = \frac{40}{5} = 8\]

Q19. Explain Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient. State the situations in which this method is suitable. (8 Marks)

1. Core Concept and Definition

Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient (\(\rho\) or \(r_s\)), developed by British psychologist Charles Spearman in 1904, is a non-parametric statistical measure of the strength and direction of monotonic association between two ranked or ordinal variables.

Unlike Karl Pearson's correlation, Spearman's rank correlation does not assume that data is normally distributed, nor does it require a strictly linear relationship; it requires only that the relationship be monotonic.

2. Mathematical Formulas and Range

  • Case 1: When Ranks are Non-Tied (Distinct Values):
    \[\rho = 1 - \frac{6 \sum d^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}\]
    where:
  • \(d = R_X - R_Y\) (Difference between paired ranks for each individual item)

  • \(n\) = Number of paired observations

  • Case 2: When Ranks are Tied (Repeated Values):

    \[\rho = 1 - \frac{6 \left[ \sum d^2 + \sum \frac{m(m^2 - 1)}{12} \right]}{n(n^2 - 1)}\]
    where \(m\) is the number of times a particular rank repeats (tied count), and tied items receive the arithmetic mean of the rank positions they occupy.

  • Bounded Range: \(-1.00 \le \rho \le +1.00\) (\(-1\) = Perfect Negative Monotonic Association, \(+1\) = Perfect Positive Monotonic Association, \(0\) = No Monotonic Association).

3. Situations in Which Spearman's Method is Suitable

  • Qualitative and Subjective Attributes: Highly suitable when variables cannot be measured on an exact quantitative scale but can be sorted or ranked hierarchically (e.g., assessing beauty, intelligence, morality, taste, or leadership qualities).
  • Non-Normal and Heavily Skewed Data: Indispensable when sample distributions violate the assumption of normality, as rank transformations neutralize distribution skewness.
  • Presence of Extreme Outliers: Resistant to extreme outliers because converting raw metric values into discrete sequential ranks (\(1, 2, \dots, n\)) bounds the distorting impact of extreme data points.
  • Monotonic Non-Linear Relationships: Measures whether variables increase or decrease together, even when the underlying mathematical function is non-linear (e.g., exponential or logarithmic curves).
  • Ordinal Scale Survey Data: Ideal for analyzing Likert-scale questionnaire responses (Strongly Agree to Strongly Disagree) in social science and market research.
  • Judge and Inter-Rater Reliability Testing: Standard tool for evaluating the level of agreement between two independent adjudicators evaluating contestants in competitions.
  • Directly Ranked Input Data: Directly applicable when input data is pre-ranked by observers without raw metric readings.

4. Real-World Example

  • Example: Two human resource managers independently evaluating and ranking 10 job candidates from 1 to 10 based on interview performance; Spearman's \(\rho\) measures the consistency of their hiring judgment.

(ii) at least one successful sale

2. Binomial Probability Mass Function (PMF)

\[(0.6)^5 = 0.07776\]
\[(0.4)^3 = 0.064\]

\[P(X = 5) = 56 \times 0.07776 \times 0.064 = 56 \times 0.00497664 = 0.27869 \approx 0.2787\]

\[\boxed{P(X = 5) = 0.2787 \quad (27.87\%)}\]

\[P(X \ge 1) = 1 - P(X = 0)\]

\[P(X = 0) = \binom{8}{0} (0.6)^0 (0.4)^8 = 1 \times 1 \times (0.4)^8 = 0.00065536\]

\[P(X \ge 1) = 1 - 0.00065536 = 0.99934464 \approx 0.9993\]

\[\boxed{P(X \ge 1) = 0.9993 \quad (99.93\%)}\]

(iii) Probability of At Most 2 Successful Sales (\(P(X \le 2)\))

\[P(X \le 2) = P(X = 0) + P(X = 1) + P(X = 2)\]

  • \(P(X = 0) = 0.00065536\)
  • \(P(X = 1) = \binom{8}{1} (0.6)^1 (0.4)^7 = 8 \times 0.6 \times 0.0016384 = 0.00786432\)
  • \(P(X = 2) = \binom{8}{2} (0.6)^2 (0.4)^6 = 28 \times 0.36 \times 0.004096 = 0.04128768\)

Summing the terms:

\[P(X \le 2) = 0.00065536 + 0.00786432 + 0.04128768 = 0.04980736 \approx 0.0498\]

\[\boxed{P(X \le 2) = 0.0498 \quad (4.98\%)}\]

Method A Method B Method C

9 13 11

  • Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): \(\mu_A = \mu_B = \mu_C\) (There is no significant difference among the mean test scores of the three teaching methods).

  • Method A: \(T_1 = 8 + 10 + 9 = 27\), sample size \(n_1 = 3\), mean \(\bar{X}_1 = 9.0\)
  • Method B: \(T_2 = 12 + 11 + 13 = 36\), sample size \(n_2 = 3\), mean \(\bar{X}_2 = 12.0\)
  • Method C: \(T_3 = 10 + 9 + 11 = 30\), sample size \(n_3 = 3\), mean \(\bar{X}_3 = 10.0\)
  • Number of groups (\(k\)) = \(3\)
  • Total observations (\(N\)) = \(n_1 + n_2 + n_3 = 3 + 3 + 3 = 9\)
  • Grand Total (\(G\)):
    \[G = T_1 + T_2 + T_3 = 27 + 36 + 30 = 93\]

  • Step 1: Correction Factor (\(CF\)):

    \[CF = \frac{G^2}{N} = \frac{(93)^2}{9} = \frac{8,649}{9} = 961\]

  • Step 2: Total Sum of Squares (\(TSS\)):

    \[\sum X^2 = (8^2 + 10^2 + 9^2) + (12^2 + 11^2 + 13^2) + (10^2 + 9^2 + 11^2)\]
    \[\sum X^2 = (64 + 100 + 81) + (144 + 121 + 169) + (100 + 81 + 121) = 245 + 434 + 302 = 981\]
    \[TSS = \sum X^2 - CF = 981 - 961 = 20\]

    \[SSB = \left( \frac{T_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{T_2^2}{n_2} + \frac{T_3^2}{n_3} \right) - CF = \left( \frac{27^2}{3} + \frac{36^2}{3} + \frac{30^2}{3} \right) - 961\]
    \[SSB = \left( \frac{729}{3} + \frac{1296}{3} + \frac{900}{3} \right) - 961 = (243 + 432 + 300) - 961 = 975 - 961 = 14\]

  • Step 4: Within-Methods Sum of Squares (\(SSW\) / Error):

    \[SSW = TSS - SSB = 20 - 14 = 6\]

4. One-Way ANOVA Summary Table

Source of Variation Sum of Squares (\(SS\)) Degrees of Freedom (\(df\)) Mean Sum of Squares (\(MS\)) Variance Ratio (\(F_{\text{calc}}\)) Critical Value (\(F_{\text{crit}}\) at \(5\%\))

Total \(TSS = 20\) \(N - 1 = 9 - 1 = 8\) — — —

5. Statistical Decision and Inference

  • Decision Rule: If \(F_{\text{calc}} > F_{\text{crit}}\), reject \(H_0\). Otherwise, accept \(H_0\).
  • Comparison:
    \[F_{\text{calculated}} = 7.00 > F_{\text{critical}} (2, 6) = 5.14\]

3. Detailed Explanation of Correlation Types

A. Classification Based on Direction of Co-Movement
  • Positive Correlation (\(0 < r \le +1\)):
  • Occurs when two variables change simultaneously in the same direction; an increase in variable \(X\) corresponds to an increase in variable \(Y\), and a decrease in \(X\) corresponds to a decrease in \(Y\).
  • On a scatter plot, observations form a cluster that slopes upward from bottom-left to top-right.
  • Example: Advertising expenditure and sales revenue; height and weight of individuals.
  • Negative Correlation (Inverse Correlation, \(-1 \le r < 0\)):
  • Occurs when two variables move in opposite directions; an increase in variable \(X\) corresponds to a decrease in variable \(Y\).
  • Scatter points slope downward from top-left to bottom-right.
  • Example: Unit price of a commodity and market demand; vehicle speed and travel time.
  • Zero Correlation (\(r = 0\)):
  • Indicates complete absence of any linear relationship between variables; variations in \(X\) provide zero information regarding variations in \(Y\).
  • Scatter points appear completely random and circular across the graph.
  • Example: Shoe size and individual IQ; student roll number and exam marks.
B. Classification Based on Number of Variables Under Study
  • Simple Correlation:
  • Examines the linear association between strictly two variables (e.g., studying the relationship between rainfall and agricultural yield in isolation).
  • Multiple Correlation:
  • Evaluates the combined, joint relationship between one dependent variable and two or more independent variables simultaneously (denoted by \(R_{1.23}\)).
  • Example: Examining how agricultural yield (\(Y\)) is jointly associated with rainfall (\(X_1\)) and fertilizer amount (\(X_2\)).
  • Partial Correlation:
  • Measures the net linear relationship between two variables while mathematically controlling and holding constant the confounding influence of one or more additional variables (denoted by \(r_{12.3}\)).
  • Example: Measuring the correlation between crop yield and fertilizer while keeping rainfall and temperature constant.
C. Classification Based on Linearity / Ratio of Variation
  • Linear Correlation:
  • Occurs when the amount of change in one variable bears a constant ratio to the amount of change in the other variable throughout the entire range of values.
  • When plotted on a Cartesian plane, points form an exact or near straight line (\(Y = a + bX\)).
  • Non-Linear (Curvilinear) Correlation:
  • Occurs when the ratio of change between variables is not constant but fluctuates at different intervals.
  • Scatter plots display curved trajectories, parabolic arcs, or U-shaped curves.
  • Example: Crop yield versus chemical fertilizer application; yield increases up to an optimal dosage, after which excess fertilizer burns crops and reduces yield.

(a) Simple Linear Regression [4 Marks]

1. Meaning & Mathematical Model

Simple Linear Regression is a supervised parametric statistical modeling technique that models and quantifies the straight-line linear relationship between a single independent predictor variable (\(X\)) and a continuous dependent response variable (\(Y\)).

\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \epsilon\]
where \(\beta_0\) represents the population \(Y\)-intercept, \(\beta_1\) denotes the regression slope (rate of change in \(Y\) per unit increase in \(X\)), and \(\epsilon\) represents the random error term.

2. Core Features
  • Ordinary Least Squares (OLS): Computes model coefficients by minimizing the sum of squared vertical prediction residuals (\(\sum e_i^2\)).
  • Model Assumptions: Assumes linearity, constant error variance (homoscedasticity), independence of observations, and normal distribution of residuals.
  • Goodness-of-Fit: Evaluated using the Coefficient of Determination (\(R^2\)), representing the proportion of total variance in \(Y\) explained by \(X\).
3. Applications & Practical Example
  • Applications: Trend extrapolation, demand forecasting, and risk analysis.
  • Example: Forecasting a retail store's annual sales revenue (\(Y\)) based on its annual advertising marketing expenditure (\(X\)).
